# Slater John Clarke bibliography

John Clarke Slater (1900–1976) was an American theoretical physicist whose work helped build the quantum-mechanical framework for atoms, molecules, and solids. He was one of the most influential theorists of his generation in condensed-matter and molecular physics, and he is remembered for both his mathematical tools and his textbooks.

## Early Life and Education

Slater was born in Oak Park, Illinois, in 1900. He studied at the University of Rochester and then at Harvard, where he completed his doctoral work in the early 1920s. This was a formative period for theoretical physics. The old quantum theory of Bohr and Sommerfeld was giving way to the new quantum mechanics of Heisenberg, Schrödinger, and Dirac, and Slater took part in that transition, working out how the new theory could be applied to real atoms and molecules.

He spent time in Europe during the late 1920s, a common path for American physicists of the era, and was in contact with the leading centers of quantum theory. He was among the early American physicists to master and promote the new mechanics.

## Career

Slater spent most of his career at Harvard and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. He became a central figure in theoretical physics at MIT, where he led the physics department for a time and helped build a strong group in solid-state theory. Over the course of his career he supervised many students and postdoctoral researchers, and his influence on the field came as much through them as through his own papers.

During the Second World War he worked on radar and microwave technology at the MIT Radiation Laboratory, one of the major wartime research efforts. This experience with applied electromagnetism and microwave devices was typical of the period, when theoretical physicists were pulled into engineering problems of enormous urgency.

## Major Scientific Contributions

Slater's contributions are numerous, and several of them remain standard tools in physics and chemistry.

**Slater determinants (1929).** Slater showed how to write a many-electron wavefunction in a form that automatically satisfies the Pauli exclusion principle, which requires the wavefunction to change sign when two electrons are exchanged. A Slater determinant arranges single-electron orbitals in a determinant, and this construction is now the basic building block of Hartree–Fock theory and much of modern quantum chemistry.

**Slater-type orbitals (1930).** Slater proposed a family of orbital functions, now called Slater-type orbitals (STOs), that decay exponentially with distance from the nucleus. They resemble the true hydrogen-like orbitals in their qualitative behavior and are convenient for calculations. Modern computational chemistry often uses Gaussian-type orbitals for efficiency, but STOs remain important conceptually and in certain methods.

**Slater's rules (1930).** Slater gave an empirical set of rules for estimating the effective nuclear charge seen by an electron in a many-electron atom, accounting for shielding by other electrons. These rules are still taught in introductory chemistry because they give quick, reasonable estimates of orbital energies and atomic sizes.

**Slater–Condon rules.** Working with Edward Condon, Slater helped formulate rules for evaluating matrix elements between configurations of many-electron atoms. These rules are central to atomic spectroscopy and to the theory of multiplet structure.

**The Slater–Pauling rule (1936–1938).** Slater, and independently Linus Pauling, related the average magnetic moment of transition-metal alloys to the average number of valence electrons. The resulting correlation, often shown as a Slater–Pauling curve, is a classic example of how electronic structure governs magnetism in materials.

**The X-alpha method (1951).** Slater developed the Hartree–Fock–Slater approach, sometimes called the Xα method, which simplifies the exchange interaction by replacing the nonlocal Hartree–Fock exchange with a local potential proportional to the electron density raised to the one-third power. The method was computationally much cheaper than full Hartree–Fock and was widely used for molecules and solids before modern density functional theory became dominant. It is an important precursor of DFT.

**Slater–Koster tight-binding scheme (1954).** With George Koster, Slater introduced a widely used parameterization of the tight-binding method for electronic band structure. The Slater–Koster approach expresses the matrix elements between atomic-like orbitals in terms of a small set of two-center integrals, making it practical to compute band structures of crystals. It remains a standard tool in solid-state physics.

## Books

Slater wrote several textbooks that shaped how generations of physicists learned quantum mechanics and solid-state theory.

*Quantum Theory of Matter* (1951) is a broad introduction to quantum mechanics and its applications to solids. *Quantum Theory of Atomic Structure* (two volumes, 1960) is a detailed treatment of atomic physics, including the Slater methods and the rules for many-electron systems. *Solid-State and Molecular Theory: A Scientific Biography* (1975) reflects on his own research path while presenting a unified view of the subject. His writing is known for clarity and for connecting formal theory to physical intuition.

## Legacy

Slater's legacy operates on several levels. Many of his concepts, including Slater determinants, STOs, the Slater rules, and the Slater–Koster scheme, are embedded so deeply in physics and chemistry that they are used routinely without much thought about their origin. His X-alpha method connects directly to the development of density functional theory, which later earned a Nobel Prize in Chemistry. His textbooks trained a generation of researchers, and he is remembered as a physicist who combined rigorous theory with a strong sense of how the mathematics maps onto materials and molecules.
